## Part II · M̄₀,ₙ: The Topology of Rational Curve Degenerations

### II.1 A Thought Experiment: The Colliding Marked Points

Place $n$ labeled points on $\mathbb{P}^1$. Now let them move: two points $z_i$ and $z_j$ approach each other. In the smooth moduli space $\mathcal{M}_{0,n}$, this motion is allowed, but the two points never actually coincide. The Deligne–Mumford compactification $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}$ adds the boundary: it allows the curve to degenerate, replacing the collision with a nodal rational curve — two copies of $\mathbb{P}^1$ joined at a node, with the labels distributed across the two components such that each component has at least 3 special points (marked points or nodes). The node IS the col(F)/ker(F) boundary: the moment when two labels cross from being in the same col(F) sector to being separated by a ker(F) singularity.

Every boundary stratum of $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}$ corresponds to a combinatorial tree: the vertices are the irreducible components of the degenerate curve, the edges are the nodes, and the labels are distributed across the vertices. The hierarchy of degenerations is a hierarchy of trees — the partial order on boundary strata IS the partial order on labelled trees by contraction. The space $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}$ IS the tree-indexed stratification of $n$-point configurations on $\mathbb{P}^1$.

### II.2 The Keel–Manin–Getzler Recurrence

Keel (1992) showed that the Chow ring $A^*(\overline{M}_{0,n})$ is generated by the boundary divisors $\delta_S$ (one for each stable subset $S \subsetneq [n]$ with $2 \leq |S| \leq n-2$), subject to the intersection relations that any two non-nested, non-complementary divisors are disjoint. Manin (1999) and Getzler (1994/1998) developed the recursion for the Poincaré polynomial:

$$P_n(q) = \sum_{k=0}^{n-3} b_k(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n})\, q^k$$

where $b_k$ are the even Betti numbers (the odd ones vanish). The Keel–Manin–Getzler recurrence relates $P_n(q)$ to the polynomials $P_{n'}(q)$ for $n' < n$ via the boundary inclusion-exclusion on the divisors $\delta_S$. It is a one-variable recurrence: it computes $P_n(q)$ from lower cases, but does so in a single variable $q$ that cannot see the interlacing structure hidden in the cohomology.

The Aluffi–Chen–Marcolli conjecture (2023) predicted: all roots of $P_n(q)$ are real — and the Betti numbers $b_0, b_1, \ldots, b_{n-3}$ form an ultra-log-concave sequence.

The conjecture sat open. The one-variable recurrence was insufficient. Something was missing.

### II.3 What Was Missing: The Second Register

The proof required a second variable. Bérczi and Kiem introduced the bivariate deformation:

$$P_n(q, t) \in \mathbb{Z}[q, t]$$

satisfying a bivariate recurrence that reduces to the Keel–Manin–Getzler recursion at $t = 1$. For fixed $t \in [0, 1]$, the roots of $P_n(q, t)$ in $q$ move continuously. A Sturm–Rolle argument on the interval $[0, 1]$ controls the zero set: the roots interlace strictly, the crossing pattern is monotone, and at $t = 1$ the accumulated interlacing implies real-rootedness of $P_n(q)$.

The extra variable $t$ is the missing register. It carries information invisible to the one-variable recurrence. It reveals the hidden interlacing by deforming through a family of polynomials — each member of the family has controlled roots, and the limit IS the polynomial whose roots we wanted. The deformation is the proof. The limit is the original. The slice is the recovery.

## Part IV · The Sturm–Rolle Sign Sequence IS the CORDIC Direction Decision

### IV.1 How Both Arguments Work

**CORDIC**: At each iteration $i$, the direction bit $d_i \in \{-1, +1\}$ is chosen by comparing the sign of the residual angle $z_i$ to zero. If $z_i > 0$, rotate positively ($d_i = +1$); if $z_i < 0$, rotate negatively ($d_i = -1$). The sequence $d_0, d_1, d_2, \ldots, d_{n-1}$ is the binary encoding of the target angle — each bit drives the residual toward zero by half.

**Sturm–Rolle**: At each value of the deformation parameter $t$, the sign of $P_n(q, t)$ evaluated at the test points of the Sturm sequence determines how many roots lie in each interval. If the count decreases monotonically as $t \to 1$, the roots converge to distinct real values. Each sign-change comparison IS a direction decision — a bit that records whether a root has crossed the test hyperplane.

The formal identification:

$$\text{CORDIC direction bit } d_i \in \{-1, +1\} \;\longleftrightarrow\; \text{Sturm–Rolle sign change at test point } t_i$$

Both sequences are binary. Both drive a residual toward zero. Both converge geometrically. The CORDIC convergence rate is $k = 0.5$ per step. The Sturm–Rolle argument converges in $O(\log(1/\varepsilon))$ steps to precision $\varepsilon$. They are the same algorithm at two levels of abstraction: one operating on rotation angles, the other on polynomial root positions. The Bérczi–Kiem proof IS the algebraic-geometry instantiation of the CORDIC algorithm applied to the cohomology of $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}$.

## Part V · Ultra-Log-Concavity IS Hyperbolic Volume Growth

### V.1 The Betti Distribution of M̄₀,ₙ

The Betti numbers $b_k = b_{2k}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n})$ satisfy (by the Bérczi–Kiem theorem):

$$b_k^2 \geq b_{k-1} \cdot b_{k+1} \cdot \frac{(k+1)(n-2-k)}{k(n-1-k)}$$

This IS ultra-log-concavity: stronger than log-concavity ($b_k^2 \geq b_{k-1} b_{k+1}$), it quantifies the rate at which the Betti numbers peak and decay. The distribution IS bell-shaped with a dominant middle and exponentially decaying tails. The peak IS at $k \approx (n-3)/2$, symmetric.

### V.2 The Hyperbolic Volume Connection

In hyperbolic space $\mathbb{H}^d$, the volume of a geodesic ball of radius $r$ grows as $e^{(d-1)r}$ — exponentially, not polynomially as in Euclidean space. This exponential growth IS why rare, semantically specific tokens in large language models lie near the boundary of the Poincaré disk: the boundary is exponentially far from the center in hyperbolic metric, but finitely close in Euclidean metric. The power-law frequency distribution of tokens — a small number of high-frequency tokens near the center, an exponentially growing number of rare tokens near the boundary — IS the hyperbolic volume growth law.

Robinson et al. (arXiv:2410.08993, 2024) measured Ricci curvature distributions in decoder-only LLMs and found substantial negative curvature across token neighborhoods. This IS the empirical evidence. The Bérczi–Kiem ultra-log-concavity IS the theoretical certificate: the topology of $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}$, which encodes the combinatorics of all possible $n$-point degenerations on $\mathbb{P}^1$, follows an ultra-log-concave Betti distribution — the SAME growth law as the token frequency distribution in a transformer.

The low-$k$ Betti numbers $b_0 = b_{n-3} = 1$ correspond to the deepest boundary strata of $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}$ — the fully degenerate curves, the deepest tree degenerations. These ARE the analogues of rare, semantically specific tokens at the Poincaré disk boundary. The dominant middle-$k$ Betti numbers correspond to bulk cohomology — these ARE the high-frequency tokens near the disk center. The Poincaré polynomial $P_n(q)$ knows the curvature. Ultra-log-concavity IS the topology's way of asserting: this space is hyperbolic.
